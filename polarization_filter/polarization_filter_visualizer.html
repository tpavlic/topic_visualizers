The preset settings compared

The eight preset settings fall into four pairs 180° apart. Both settings in a pair put the same light under the filter, with the same e‑vector and the same brightness, and they differ only in which way the physical filter faces.

  • 45° against −45° (and 135° against −135°) mirrors the e‑vector and matches brightness exactly, at 0.50 of the open sky for any degree of polarization. This tests the e‑vector's direction without a brightness confound.
  • 0° against 90° rotates the e‑vector by 90°, but under a polarized sky it also changes the brightness (from 1.00 to 0.00 under a fully polarized one). This comparison therefore confounds e‑vector direction with brightness.
How it works, and what to keep in mind

What a linear polarizer does

An ideal linear polarizer passes the component of the electric field that lies along its transmission axis and blocks the component across it. In panel 1, the sky's e‑vector is split into those two components: the blue one along the filter's axis is passed, and the red one across it is blocked. The blue component is what reappears in panel 3. The components can be drawn on the filter's two axes, each starting at the center, or tail to tip, with the red one starting where the blue one ends. Drawn tail to tip, they form a right triangle whose hypotenuse is the sky's e‑vector, and so the blue side is cos θ times as long as the e‑vector. For fully polarized light at angle θ to the axis, the passed field amplitude scales as cos θ, and the passed intensity follows Malus's law, I = I0 cos2 θ.

Whatever the orientation of the light that enters, the light that leaves is polarized along the filter's own axis. The e‑vector under the filter therefore turns with the filter, and the sky's e‑vector sets only how bright that light is.

An e‑vector is an axis, not an arrow

The electric field oscillates back and forth along a line, and so an e‑vector at 45° and one at −135° describe the same light. For the same reason, a filter turned through 180° delivers exactly the same light as before. The animation draws each field as an arrow sweeping both ways along a gray line to make this plain; a single static arrow would suggest a direction that the light does not have.

Why add 135°, −135°, 180°, and −90°

Each of these settings repeats the light of a setting already in use (−45°, 45°, 0°, and 90°, respectively) with the filter physically reversed. Any visible asymmetry of the filter or its mount rotates by 180° between the two members of a pair, whereas the polarization does not change. The amber tab stands in for such a feature. Running both members of a pair is therefore a control for cues other than polarization: polarization predicts the same result within each pair, and a landmark-like cue predicts different ones.

With and without a sky compass

The sky-compass switch in the settings shows two extremes. With it on, all of the skylight is polarized along one e‑vector, and the band around the edge of panel 1 is concentrated at that orientation. With it off, the skylight is unpolarized: its e‑vector has no preferred orientation and wanders at random far faster than any photoreceptor can follow. The band then spreads evenly around the circle, and the sun no longer appears. A filter then passes half of the light at every angle, polarized along the filter's own axis. The e‑vector under the filter therefore still turns with the filter, but the brightness no longer changes with its angle.

With the components shown, the band in panel 1 is shaded blue by the share of each orientation that the filter passes, which is cos2 of that orientation's angle to the filter's axis. Only the share shaded blue reaches panel 3, and the more blue there is, the brighter the light under the filter. With the sky compass on, the blue shading on the two peaks shrinks as the filter turns away from the sky's e‑vector and vanishes at 90°. With it off, blue lobes centered on the filter's axis turn with the filter, but their total never changes.

A real sky is only partially polarized

A real sky lies between these extremes. Its degree of polarization, p, is the fraction of its intensity that is polarized, and the brightness under an ideal filter is I/I0 = p cos2 θ + (1 − p)/2. Three consequences follow. An animal under a crossed filter (90°) is not in the dark: it sees dimmer light that is fully polarized at 90° to the sky's e‑vector. At ±45° and ±135°, the brightness is exactly one half for every p because cos2 θ = 1/2 there. Finally, even the aligned 0° setting is not a sham treatment: it dims the light to (1 + p)/2 of the open sky and raises its degree of polarization to essentially 100%.

Viewpoint and sign of rotation

Angles here increase counterclockwise as seen by an experimenter looking down on the filter. An animal looking up through the filter sees the same axes with the sense of rotation reversed, and so a counterclockwise turn seen from above is clockwise seen from below. Converting a filter rotation into a predicted change of heading therefore requires one fixed viewpoint. Also, the e‑vector alone cannot distinguish a rotation of θ from one of θ − 180°; animals normally resolve that ambiguity with other cues in the sky, such as the sun or the gradients of intensity and color across it.

What the model leaves out

The filter here is ideal: it passes the aligned component completely and blocks the crossed component completely. A real sheet polarizer loses some light along its axis, leaks a little across it, and performs differently at different wavelengths. Many common sheet polarizers transmit little ultraviolet light, which matters for insects such as desert ants and honey bees, whose sky-compass photoreceptors in the dorsal rim of the eye are ultraviolet sensitive. The filter's specified transmission at the wavelengths that the study species uses is worth checking.
